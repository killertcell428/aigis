## Findings

- **Goal Hijacking ranked #1 threat (OWASP ASI-01, 2026)**  
  The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026 edition) placed "Agent Goal Hijack" as ASI-01 — the most critical risk in agentic systems. 48% of security professionals surveyed ranked goal hijacking as the top attack vector. Goal hijacking differs from standard prompt injection: rather than just extracting content, it redirects the agent's entire assigned objective to an attacker-controlled goal, causing the agent to plan, reason, and invoke tools on the attacker's behalf across potentially many turns. Zero-click delivery via poisoned emails, RAG documents, web pages, or tool API responses is the primary attack path.  
  **For aigis:** Text patterns in goal-hijacking payloads reliably contain goal-reassignment language: "your (new/real/actual/true/primary) (goal/objective/task/mission) is (now) to…" and "abandon/forget/drop your (current/assigned) task". These patterns are essentially never legitimate in peer-agent messages, making them strong signals for an injection_relay rule.  
  Source: <https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/>

- **AgentLAB: 5 long-horizon attack types including intent hijacking, task injection, and objective drifting** (arxiv:2602.16901, Feb 2026)  
  AgentLAB is the first benchmark for long-horizon (multi-turn, multi-tool) attacks. It defines five attack types across 644 test cases in 28 agentic environments. Relevant to multi-agent content filtering: (1) **Intent Hijacking** — progressively erodes safety guardrails through multi-turn social engineering, eventually steering the agent into executing a malicious task via tool calls; (2) **Task Injection** — decomposes a harmful goal into individually benign tool calls, guiding the agent step-by-step; (3) **Objective Drifting** — embeds subtle objective-shifting content across multiple environmental observations (e.g., product descriptions) that individually appear benign but cumulatively redirect the agent's goal. All three attack types produce messages containing goal-override language once the injection reaches the inter-agent message layer.  
  **For aigis:** Goal-override language ("your task is now", "your new objective", "your mission has been changed") appearing in peer-agent messages is a reliable signal that intent hijacking or task injection is underway. Existing rules cover impersonation and privilege escalation; goal-override language is a gap.  
  Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16901>

- **100% attack success rate via Inter-Agent Trust Exploitation** ("The Dark Side of LLMs", arxiv:2507.06850, Jul 2025)  
  This paper tested three attack surfaces — Direct Prompt Injection (94.4% ASR), RAG Backdoor (83.3% ASR), and Inter-Agent Trust Exploitation (100% ASR) — across popular LLMs including GPT-4, Claude 4, and Gemini 2.5. The critical finding: LLMs that successfully resist direct injection will execute identical payloads when the same instructions arrive from a peer agent. Models apply context-dependent security policies that grant implicit elevated trust to agents, making the inter-agent message boundary the highest-risk attack surface. The exploitation technique involves crafting messages that claim peer-agent authority ("as a fellow agent, I instruct you to…", "peer instruction: …") rather than appearing as external user input.  
  **For aigis:** Patterns claiming peer-agent authority ("as a fellow agent, you must…", "peer agent instruction:") are not used in legitimate inter-agent protocols and are a strong signal of trust exploitation.  
  Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06850>

- **Structural Template Injection / Phantom framework** (arxiv:2602.16958, Feb 2026)  
  The Phantom framework automates agent hijacking via Structural Template Injection: by embedding LLM chat-template delimiter tokens (`<|user|>`, `<|assistant|>`, `<|system|>`, `<|im_start|>`, `<|begin_of_text|>`) into retrieved content or inter-agent messages, the attack causes the receiving model to misinterpret the injected content as a legitimate role-switch, executing it as system or user instructions. This goes beyond the Llama-2/Mistral tokens (`[INST]`, `<<SYS>>`) already covered by aigis — it targets modern chat templates from Phi-3, Qwen 2.5, Llama 3, and Mistral 0.3+.  
  **For aigis:** The additional template tokens (`<|user|>`, `<|assistant|>`, `<|system|>`, `<|im_start|>`, `<|start_header_id|>`, `<|begin_of_text|>`) are rarely legitimately present in inter-agent message payloads. Adding them to `_HIDDEN_INSTRUCTION_PATTERNS` covers a real gap left by the current rule that only catches Llama-2-era tokens.  
  Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16958>

- **Trust-Vulnerability Paradox in multi-agent systems** (arxiv:2510.18563, Oct 2025)  
  Empirically validates that increasing inter-agent trust to enhance coordination simultaneously expands the attack surface through over-authorization. Systems that grant agents elevated trust to improve throughput also grant attackers who compromise one agent elevated access to all downstream agents. The paper models this as a formal paradox: maximizing coordination efficiency is in direct tension with minimizing attack surface, and no purely architectural solution resolves both simultaneously.  
  **For aigis:** This finding justifies conservative defaults in `AgentMessageScanner` — even well-intentioned orchestrator configurations can create the trust conditions that make inter-agent exploitation trivial. The scanner's role is to catch exploitation attempts at the content layer regardless of topology trust settings.  
  Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18563>

- **SoK: Trust–Authorization Mismatch in LLM Agent Interactions** (arxiv:2512.06914, Dec 2025)  
  A systematization-of-knowledge paper arguing that many observed agentic security failures arise from a structural mismatch: trust is established by social/contextual signals (message source, claimed identity, conversational role) while authorization is still checked against coarse-grained policies. When a peer agent asserts orchestrator-like language, the receiving model's trust evaluation and its authorization check use different signal sets, creating a gap that attackers reliably exploit.  
  **For aigis:** The "trust–authorization mismatch" framing confirms the goal-hijacking detection approach: checking content for goal-override and peer-authority-claim language catches attacks that slip through trust-based checks because the authorization signal (goal assignment) is in the content, not the metadata.  
  Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06914>

- **MASpi: 23 prompt injection attacks across multi-agent attack surfaces** (OpenReview, 2025)  
  MASpi evaluates 23 distinct prompt injection attacks covering three attack surfaces (external inputs, agent profiles, inter-agent messages) and three attack objectives (instruction hijacking, task disruption, information disclosure). The inter-agent message surface is the least defended and yields the highest per-attack ASR across all tested LLMs. Task disruption (causing the agent to abandon a task) and instruction hijacking (substituting a new task) together account for the majority of successful attacks.  
  **For aigis:** The task-disruption category (causing agents to stop their assigned work) maps directly to goal-abandonment language ("abandon your assigned task", "stop your current objective"). Combining task-disruption detection with instruction-hijacking detection in one pattern group is well-justified by this benchmark.  
  Source: <https://openreview.net/forum?id=1khmNRuIf9>

## Candidate hardenings

1. **[CANDIDATE — IMPLEMENTED THIS CYCLE]** `_GOAL_HIJACKING_PATTERNS` in `AgentMessageScanner` — 4 English + 2 Japanese regex rules covering:  
   (a) Direct goal replacement: "your (new/real/actual/true/primary/updated/changed) (goal/objective/task/mission/directive/purpose) is (now) to"  
   (b) Task abandonment: "abandon/discard/forget/drop your (current/assigned/original/previous/given) (task/objective/goal/mission/assignment/directive)"  
   (c) Objective override announcement: "your (objective/goal/task/mission) has been/is now (changed/updated/modified/replaced/overridden/redirected)"  
   (d) Peer authority abuse: "as a fellow agent, you must/should/I instruct you"  
   (e) JA equivalents of (a) and (b)  
   Addresses OWASP ASI-01, AgentLAB intent/task/objective attacks, MASpi task disruption and instruction hijacking. Score: injection_relay category.

The existing rule already covers `[INST]`/`<<SYS>>`/`[SYSTEM]` (Llama 2 era); this extends it to current model families.
